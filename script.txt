May 1498. Four wooden ships reach Calicut, India. They've found a sea road to the spice coast. At first, a polite welcome.
Four years later, Vasco da Gama returns with about twenty armed ships. Off the Malabar coast he seizes the Miri, a ship of pilgrims coming home from Mecca. The cargo is looted. Roughly four hundred people are aboard. He sets it on fire.
Then comes a new rule: buy a Portuguese pass, a cartaz, or lose your ship. In 1510, Goa falls. It stays Portuguese for about four hundred and fifty years.
The Dutch push in next. In 1663 they take Cochin. Their trick? Indian cloth that buys spices across Asia.
1757. Plassey. Clive has about three thousand men. The Nawab has fifty thousand, on paper. But his commander Mir Jafar has already made a deal, and stands his army down. Clive even forges a second treaty, on red paper, to fool a go-between. Murshidabad's treasury is emptied. Mir Jafar owes the Company over seventeen million rupees. The Nawab? Dead within ten days.
In 1765 the Company gets Bengal's tax rights. Then the rains fail, and little relief comes. In 1769 and 1770, an estimated seven to ten million people die.
1849. A ten-year-old Maharaja, Duleep Singh, signs away Punjab. Article Three sends the Koh-i-Noor to Queen Victoria. It's shown in London in 1851, then recut to about a hundred and five carats.
Economist Utsa Patnaik puts the total drain at forty-five trillion dollars, a figure that is contested. What's clear is that the sea road opened in 1498 changed who controlled India's wealth. Follow for part two.
